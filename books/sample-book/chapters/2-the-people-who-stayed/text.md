
# Chapter 2 — The People Who Stayed

At eight in the morning, San Rosario had traffic.

A white Ford pickup drove down Main Street.

Ten minutes later, it came back.

That was apparently rush hour.

I was sitting outside the hotel drinking coffee when Walter pointed toward it.

"That's Ruth."

"The woman whose husband died?"

"Yep."

"Where's she going?"

"Post office."

I looked down the street.

"I didn't see a post office."

"You wouldn't."

This was becoming a recurring feature of San Rosario.

Things existed, but only if somebody told you where they were.

The post office occupied the front room of a small adobe house three blocks from the hotel. There was no flag outside, no blue mailbox, and no sign except for a sheet of paper taped inside the window:

**SAN ROSARIO POST OFFICE**

**10:00–12:00**

**MONDAY / WEDNESDAY / FRIDAY**

It was Wednesday.

I walked over at ten.

Inside, I found Ruth.

She was seventy-eight years old, five feet tall, and wearing a turquoise blouse, blue jeans, and bright red sneakers.

She looked at my camera.

"You're the photographer."

News traveled quickly among twenty-five people.

"Apparently."

"Walter told me."

"Of course he did."

"He says you're writing something."

"I might."

"Then he'll lie to you."

I laughed.

"About what?"

"Everything."

From behind a row of post office boxes, a woman said, "That's not true."

This was Elena, San Rosario's postmaster.

She was fifty-two and had driven thirty miles from another town to open a post office serving a population smaller than most kindergarten classes.

Ruth placed two envelopes on the counter.

"How's your mother?"

"Mean."

"Good."

"Doctor says she'll live forever."

"That's unfortunate."

Elena stamped the envelopes.

Neither woman smiled.

I was beginning to understand the local sense of humor.

---

Ruth had lived in San Rosario since 1969.

She arrived when she was twenty-one years old after marrying a man named Thomas Delgado.

"He told me it was a town," she said.

We were sitting in her kitchen later that morning.

"He lied."

"You didn't like it?"

"I hated it."

She poured iced tea into two glasses.

"I cried every day for six months."

Ruth had grown up in San Antonio.

She had been accustomed to stores, restaurants, movie theaters, neighbors, buses, noise.

San Rosario had none of these things.

"What changed?"

"I got busy."

Thomas worked maintaining equipment at a ranch north of town. Ruth taught at the school.

They had three children.

Then five.

"Five?"

"Three ours. Two everybody else's."

She explained that two children from another family had effectively moved into their house during high school.

"Nobody officially decided anything. They just kept showing up for dinner."

"Did their parents mind?"

"They were the ones sending them."

Her refrigerator was covered with photographs.

Children.

Grandchildren.

Great-grandchildren.

Graduations.

Weddings.

Christmases.

One photograph showed Ruth and Thomas standing beside a camper somewhere in Colorado.

Another showed them on a beach.

"Corpus Christi?"

"Hawaii."

I looked at her.

"You went to Hawaii?"

"Twice."

This surprised me for reasons I couldn't explain.

Perhaps I had already begun turning the people of San Rosario into characters who existed only inside the town.

Ruth noticed.

"You thought we didn't leave?"

"No."

"You did."

"Maybe a little."

She smiled.

"That's the problem with people who come here."

"What?"

"They think staying means you never went anywhere."

---

Thomas had died six months earlier.

His chair was still at the kitchen table.

His boots remained beside the back door.

There was a photograph of him on the refrigerator holding an enormous fish.

"Was that around here?"

"Canada."

"Of course."

She laughed.

Ruth had traveled more than I had.

"Why stay here now?"

She looked through the kitchen window.

Her yard was full of flowers.

I hadn't noticed them from the road.

Roses grew along the fence. There were sunflowers beside a shed and something purple climbing a trellis.

"Because that's my garden."

I waited for more.

There wasn't any.

Later, walking back toward the hotel, I thought about Walter's answer from the night before.

People wanted reasons.

Perhaps places didn't always require them.

---

Around noon I met Gary.

This happened accidentally.

I was photographing the remains of the old gas station when a battered Jeep pulled onto the shoulder.

A man leaned out the window.

"You the guy counting people?"

"I didn't know I was."

"Walter says I don't live here."

I lowered the camera.

"Gary?"

"Goddamn right."

He parked.

Gary was sixty-three, heavily bearded, and wearing a camouflage hat advertising a brand of tractor.

"I live six miles out."

"So I've heard."

"Same ZIP code."

"That's compelling evidence."

"Thank you."

"Walter disagrees."

"Walter's an idiot."

Walter, I would later discover, considered Gary one of his closest friends.

Gary lived in a thirty-foot travel trailer on land his father had purchased in 1974.

He had solar panels, a water tank, two generators, satellite internet, and enough canned food to survive what he described as "at least the first year of the collapse."

"What collapse?"

He shrugged.

"Pick one."

Gary was not exactly a survivalist.

He simply disliked depending on systems he couldn't repair.

He had spent thirty years working as an electrician in Houston before returning to San Rosario.

"I got tired of traffic."

"So you moved somewhere without any."

"Exactly."

He opened the back of the Jeep.

Inside were groceries.

"You going back to town?"

"I'm already in town."

He pointed at me.

"Write that down."

I promised I would.

---

Gary's property was farther into the desert than I expected.

The road was barely a road.

It crossed two dry creek beds and passed through three cattle gates.

At one point Gary stopped his Jeep, got out, and moved a tortoise from the road.

"Desert tortoise," he said.

"Rare?"

"Not if you know where they are."

His trailer stood beneath a metal roof surrounded by mesquite trees.

There was a workshop beside it.

The workshop was immaculate.

Tools covered one wall in perfect rows. Plastic bins were labeled with electrical components, plumbing fittings, bolts, screws, radio parts, and things I couldn't identify.

"You could rebuild civilization from here."

"That's the idea."

Behind the workshop stood an enormous radio antenna.

"What's that?"

"Ham."

"Radio?"

"No, sandwich."

I stared at him.

Gary sighed.

"Yes, radio."

I was getting better at this.

---

We spent the afternoon driving through the desert.

Gary knew where everything had been.

Not where it was.

Where it had been.

There had been a ranch house near a dry creek.

Gone.

There had been a water tower near the railroad.

Gone.

There had been a small settlement of Mexican mine workers south of San Rosario.

Gone.

At each location, Gary stopped and pointed.

Sometimes there was nothing to photograph.

That became interesting in itself.

A landscape could contain memory without containing evidence.

"This used to be the baseball field."

We were standing in a flat patch of desert.

There was no fence.

No bleachers.

No bases.

Nothing.

"You're kidding."

"Nope."

"Where was home plate?"

Gary walked twenty yards.

"About here."

I took a photograph.

It looked like a photograph of dirt.

Maybe that was appropriate.

---

The school was easier.

It still existed.

The front doors were chained, but Gary had a key.

"Why do you have a key?"

"I have keys to everything."

This was not an answer, but I was learning not to insist.

Inside, the hallway smelled of dust.

Children's drawings remained pinned to one bulletin board.

The colors had faded almost completely.

A construction-paper sun.

A horse.

Something that might have been a dinosaur.

The final class had left the building nearly thirty years earlier.

Yet their handwriting remained.

In one classroom, multiplication tables were written on a chalkboard.

I photographed them.

Gary stood in the doorway.

"You went here?"

"Yep."

"What grade?"

"All of them."

The school had combined classes.

First and second together.

Third through fifth.

Later, as enrollment declined, nearly everyone shared the same room.

"Good school?"

"Best."

"Everybody says that about their school."

"Everybody else is wrong."

He walked down the hallway.

---

Behind the school was a basketball court.

The hoops had no nets.

Weeds grew through cracks in the asphalt.

Gary picked up a flat basketball lying near the fence.

"Probably been here twenty years."

He bounced it.

It hit the pavement with a dull thud and rolled away.

"Still terrible," he said.

"Were you good?"

"No."

"Then maybe it's not the ball."

He looked at me.

"You staying another night?"

"I think so."

"Then we're drinking."

Apparently I had passed some kind of test.

---

At six o'clock, people began appearing at the hotel.

First Gary.

Then Elena, the postmaster.

Then Ruth.

Then a rancher named Miguel and his wife Teresa.

Walter carried a folding table onto the porch.

Someone produced a cooler.

Someone else brought enchiladas.

Nobody had invited me.

Nobody seemed surprised that I was there.

Within half an hour, eleven people had gathered.

Almost half the population of San Rosario.

Or slightly less than half, depending on Gary.

"You counting him?" Walter asked.

Gary raised a beer.

"Same ZIP code."

The argument resumed.

It was apparently ceremonial.

Nobody expected resolution.

---

As darkness settled over town, the porch became the center of San Rosario.

Stories circulated.

A mountain lion had been seen near the highway.

Someone's well pump had failed.

Elena's mother had threatened a nurse with a cane.

A storm might arrive Friday.

Miguel needed help repairing a fence.

Ruth had too many tomatoes.

This last problem was solved immediately.

Bags of tomatoes appeared.

I was given one.

"I don't have a kitchen," I said.

Ruth looked offended.

"Everybody needs tomatoes."

I accepted them.

---

At some point, Walter brought out an old photograph.

It showed the same porch.

Maybe fifty people stood in front of the hotel.

Men in suits.

Women in dresses.

Children sitting cross-legged in the dirt.

"Fourth of July," Walter said.

"What year?"

"1951."

Gary leaned over.

"That's my dad."

He pointed to a boy near the edge of the photograph.

"You sure?"

"No."

Walter laughed.

The photograph passed from person to person.

Everyone found someone.

A parent.

An uncle.

A teacher.

A neighbor.

A person whose name nobody remembered.

The town in the photograph was full.

The town around us was almost empty.

But the porch was not.

---

I stepped into the street to take a picture.

Eleven people sat beneath the yellow porch light.

Behind them, HOTEL ROSARIO was barely visible.

I raised the camera.

Nobody posed.

That was better.

Gary was talking.

Ruth was laughing.

Walter was opening another beer.

Miguel had a plate balanced on one knee.

Teresa was pointing at something in the old photograph.

I pressed the shutter.

For the first time since arriving, I understood what I had been photographing incorrectly.

I had come to San Rosario because I thought it was a story about a disappearing town.

Empty buildings.

Abandoned roads.

A school without children.

A railroad without tracks.

Those things were real.

But they weren't the story.

The story was sitting on the porch.

---

Later, after everyone had gone home, Walter and I remained outside.

The temperature had fallen again.

Lightning flickered somewhere beyond the mountains.

"How long do you think the town has?" I asked.

Walter frowned.

"Until what?"

"Until nobody lives here."

"Don't know."

"Twenty years?"

"Maybe."

"Ten?"

"Maybe."

He finished his beer.

"People have been saying San Rosario is dying since I was a kid."

"Isn't it?"

"Sure."

He stood and carried his bottle inside.

At the doorway he stopped.

"So am I."

Then he disappeared into the hotel.

I sat alone on the porch.

Across the street, the abandoned filling station was barely visible in the moonlight.

A light remained on in Ruth's kitchen.

Farther away, another glowed in Miguel and Teresa's house.

Beyond town, somewhere six miles into the desert, Gary's trailer probably counted.

The wind picked up.

A tumbleweed rolled across Main Street with such perfect timing that I actually laughed.

It seemed offensively cliché.

Then it vanished into the darkness.

---

I woke sometime after midnight to thunder.

Rain hammered the hotel roof.

I opened the window.

The desert smelled completely different.

Dust had become earth.

Water poured from the gutters and ran through the street.

Lightning illuminated the abandoned buildings in white flashes.

For perhaps an hour, San Rosario disappeared behind rain.

Then the storm moved east.

The silence returned.

I went back to sleep.

---

At sunrise, I walked to the edge of town.

Water filled the dry creek.

Birds were everywhere.

The mountains were sharp and clear.

The desert that had been brown the day before seemed almost green.

I set up the tripod beside the old railroad grade.

From there I could see the whole town.

The hotel.

The church.

The school.

Ruth's house.

Main Street.

Twenty-five people.

Twenty-six with Gary.

I waited for the light.

Behind me, tires crunched on gravel.

Gary's Jeep stopped.

He rolled down the window.

"You leaving?"

"Tomorrow."

He nodded.

"Good."

"Good?"

"Means you can help Miguel with his fence."

Then he drove away.

I watched the Jeep disappear down the road.

Apparently I had plans.

I turned back toward San Rosario.

The morning sun reached the hotel first.

Its windows turned gold.

Then the light moved slowly down the street, touching one building after another.

I raised the camera.

And waited.
