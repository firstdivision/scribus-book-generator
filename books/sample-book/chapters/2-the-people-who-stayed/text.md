# Chapter 2 — The People Who Stayed

At eight in the morning, San Rosario experienced what passed for traffic. A white Ford pickup came down Main Street, turned near the old filling station, and disappeared behind the church. Ten minutes later it returned in the opposite direction. Walter, sitting beside me on the hotel porch, identified the driver as Ruth Delgado and explained that she was going to the post office. I had walked nearly every street in town the previous evening without seeing anything that looked like a post office, which Walter found unsurprising. San Rosario contained several institutions that were visible only if someone told you where to look.

The post office occupied the front room of a small adobe house three blocks from the hotel. There was no flag, no blue mailbox, and no government-looking sign beside the road. The only indication of its purpose was a sheet of white paper taped inside the window identifying it as the SAN ROSARIO POST OFFICE and listing its hours as ten until noon on Monday, Wednesday, and Friday. Elena Ruiz, the postmaster, lived thirty miles away and made the drive three mornings a week to serve a town with fewer residents than many suburban cul-de-sacs. The arrangement seemed absurd until I spent time there. During the two hours the office was open, nearly everyone who entered stayed to talk. Mail was only one of the things being exchanged.

That was where I properly met Ruth. She was seventy-eight years old, barely five feet tall, and wore a turquoise blouse, jeans, and bright red sneakers. She already knew who I was. Walter had apparently described me as "the photographer who's writing something," which was generous considering I had not yet decided whether I was writing anything. Ruth warned me immediately that Walter would lie about everything, while Elena, sorting envelopes behind the counter, objected that this was unfair. The resulting argument had the comfortable rhythm of something repeated many times before. Nobody seemed remotely irritated. In a town this small, disagreement was not evidence of distance between people. Often it was evidence of familiarity.

Ruth had arrived in San Rosario in 1969 at the age of twenty-one, newly married to Thomas Delgado, who had grown up nearby and found work maintaining equipment on a ranch north of town. She had grown up in San Antonio and initially regarded San Rosario as something between an exile and a practical joke. She was accustomed to grocery stores, restaurants, buses, movie theaters, neighbors, and the possibility of leaving the house without knowing everyone she might encounter. San Rosario offered none of those things. The nearest supermarket required planning. Entertainment was whatever somebody organized. Privacy existed, but anonymity did not. She told me later, while we sat at her kitchen table, that she had cried almost every day during her first six months there.

What changed was not the town but the amount of life Ruth accumulated inside it. She began teaching at the school. She and Thomas had three children. Two more teenagers from another family gradually became permanent fixtures at their dinner table and eventually, without anyone formally deciding it, members of the household. There were school programs, illnesses, birthdays, broken cars, Little League games in neighboring towns, church dinners, livestock escaping from fences, and the constant improvisations required when the nearest convenient solution to a problem was sixty miles away. San Rosario stopped feeling empty because Ruth no longer had enough free time to notice its emptiness.

Her kitchen refrigerator was covered with photographs held in place by magnets collected from places around the country. There were children, grandchildren, great-grandchildren, graduations, weddings, Christmas mornings, fishing trips, and family reunions. One photograph showed Ruth and Thomas standing beside a camper in Colorado. Another showed them on a beach in Hawaii, where they had traveled twice. This surprised me, and Ruth immediately understood why. Visitors often imagined that the people who remained in San Rosario had remained because they had never seen anywhere else. The assumption irritated her. She and Thomas had traveled throughout the United States. They had visited Mexico, Canada, and most of the western national parks. Staying in San Rosario had never meant being trapped there. It simply meant that after leaving, they kept choosing to come back.

Thomas had died six months before my visit. His chair remained at the kitchen table. His boots were still beside the back door, neatly aligned against the wall. There were photographs of him everywhere once I knew which face to look for. In one he held an enormous fish in Canada. In another he stood beside a truck with three children sitting on the hood. Ruth spoke about his death without trying to disguise the hole it had left in her life, but she also resisted the idea that losing him had transformed San Rosario into a place she ought to leave. Her house was there. Her garden was there. The roses along the fence had been planted more than thirty years earlier. Sunflowers grew beside the shed. Purple morning glories climbed a trellis Thomas had built from scrap lumber. When people asked why she stayed, she sometimes pointed to the garden because it was easier than explaining that a life assembled over fifty years could not be packed into a moving truck merely because one of the people who built it was gone.

I met Gary shortly after noon while photographing the remains of the old filling station. A battered Jeep pulled onto the shoulder and stopped beside me. The driver leaned through the open window and asked whether I was "the guy counting people." Before I could answer, he announced that Walter was wrong and that he absolutely lived in San Rosario. This was my introduction to the town's most persistent demographic controversy. Gary lived approximately six miles outside the recognized cluster of buildings, on land his father had purchased in 1974. His mailing address said San Rosario. His driver's license said San Rosario. His property taxes went to the same county. Walter's counterargument consisted largely of pointing out that if someone lived six miles outside San Rosario, the phrase "outside San Rosario" seemed decisive.

Gary was sixty-three, heavily bearded, and recently retired after three decades as an electrician in Houston. He had returned to his father's property because, as he put it, he had spent enough of his life sitting in traffic. The travel trailer where he lived stood beneath a large metal roof surrounded by mesquite trees. Beside it was a workshop that looked startlingly organized compared with everything else I had seen in the desert. Tools covered one wall in precise rows. Shelves held labeled bins containing electrical components, plumbing fittings, bolts, screws, radio parts, automotive parts, and enough miscellaneous hardware to repair almost anything short of an aircraft. Solar panels provided much of his electricity. A water tank stood behind the trailer. Two generators provided backup power. A tall amateur-radio antenna rose above the property.

Gary's self-sufficiency was less ideological than practical. He did not talk about escaping society or preparing for some imminent catastrophe. He simply disliked depending on systems he could not fix. Living six miles outside a town that was itself more than an hour from most services rewarded that philosophy. If a water pump failed, he repaired it. If an electrical component burned out, he probably had another one in a labeled plastic bin. If a vehicle refused to start, he opened the hood rather than reaching for a phone. San Rosario had always demanded a certain amount of mechanical competence from its residents, and Gary had turned that requirement into something approaching an art form.

That afternoon he drove me through the country surrounding town. Gary possessed a mental map containing not only what existed but what had once existed. A patch of desert beside a dry creek had been a ranch house. A concrete slab hidden beneath grass had supported a water tank. Several depressions in the earth marked buildings associated with the old mine. South of town, a settlement of Mexican mine workers had once contained dozens of small houses. Almost nothing remained. At each location Gary stopped the Jeep, walked into what looked to me like empty landscape, and pointed toward invisible structures with absolute confidence. His memories supplied walls, doors, fences, roads, and people where my camera recorded only grass and dirt.

The strangest stop was the old baseball field. We stood in a broad, flat patch of desert perhaps half a mile from town. There was no backstop, no fence, no bleachers, and no recognizable diamond. Gary walked into the weeds and stopped where he believed home plate had been. Games had once drawn people from ranches and mining settlements throughout the area. Families brought food. Children ran along the sidelines. Cars lined the road. By the time Gary was a teenager, the field was already deteriorating, but games were still occasionally played there. Now the only evidence was the unusual flatness of the ground and the memories of people old enough to remember where the bases belonged.

The school offered more tangible evidence. Its front doors were chained, but Gary had a key, something he explained only by saying that he had keys to everything. Inside, the air smelled of dust, dry wood, and old paper. Children's drawings remained pinned to a bulletin board in the hallway. The construction paper had faded until reds became pink and blues became gray. A yellow sun occupied one sheet. Another showed a horse beneath a disproportionately large rainbow. In one classroom, multiplication tables remained written on a chalkboard. Desks had been pushed against the walls, but a few textbooks remained on shelves. The building had been closed for nearly thirty years, yet it retained enough detail to feel temporarily vacant rather than abandoned.

Gary had attended every grade there. When enrollment was larger, students had been divided into several classrooms. Later, as the town shrank, grades were combined. By the final years, much of the school operated almost like the one-room schools of an earlier century. Older students worked independently while a teacher instructed younger ones. Everyone knew everyone else's siblings, parents, strengths, failures, and disciplinary history. Gary insisted it had been an excellent education. Whether that was objectively true hardly mattered. The school had given generations of San Rosario children a shared institution, and its closing represented something larger than the inconvenience of a long bus ride. A town without a school no longer expects many children.

By early evening, people began gathering at the Hotel Rosario. Nobody had announced a party, yet within half an hour the porch held eleven people. Gary arrived first, followed by Elena from the post office. Ruth appeared carrying a pan of enchiladas. Miguel Alvarez, who ran cattle north of town, arrived with his wife Teresa and a cooler. Folding chairs materialized from somewhere inside the hotel. Walter brought out a table. Plates appeared. Beer appeared. A bag of tomatoes from Ruth's garden circulated until nearly everyone had taken some. When I explained that my hotel room lacked a kitchen, Ruth gave me tomatoes anyway, apparently regarding access to cooking equipment as irrelevant to whether a person needed vegetables.

The gathering transformed the hotel porch. During the day it had seemed like part of an old building preserved beyond its expected life. That evening it became the center of the town. Conversations moved between subjects without introductions. Someone had seen a mountain lion near the highway. Elena's mother had threatened a nurse with her cane. Miguel needed help repairing a section of fence damaged during the previous storm. A well pump was making a noise it shouldn't. Someone's granddaughter had been accepted to college. Rain might arrive Friday. The topics were ordinary, which was exactly what made them important. San Rosario's continued existence did not depend on grand acts of preservation. It depended on people knowing whose pump was broken, who needed help with a fence, and who had too many tomatoes.

At some point Walter carried an old photograph outside. It showed the hotel porch in 1951, crowded with perhaps fifty people celebrating the Fourth of July. Men wore button-down shirts and hats. Women stood in summer dresses. Children sat in the dirt in front. The photograph moved from hand to hand as everyone tried to identify relatives. Gary believed one of the boys might be his father. Walter thought an older man near the door was an uncle. Ruth recognized a woman who had taught at the school before she arrived in town. Several faces remained unidentified. Their names had disappeared even though their images had survived.

I stepped into the street and photographed the porch from across Main Street. Nobody arranged themselves for the camera. Ruth was laughing at something Teresa had said. Gary was halfway through a story. Miguel balanced a plate on one knee. Walter leaned against a porch column. Behind them the faded HOTEL ROSARIO sign was just visible in the last daylight. It was the first photograph I made in San Rosario that felt as though it contained the thing I had actually come to find, even though I hadn't understood what that thing was when I arrived.

I had assumed San Rosario was a story about disappearance. The empty school, abandoned railroad, collapsed houses, and obsolete population sign all supported that interpretation. They were visually irresistible evidence of decline. Yet decline was only half of what was happening. The other half was persistence, and persistence was much harder to photograph. It looked like eleven people eating enchiladas on a hotel porch. It looked like a post office kept open six hours a week. It looked like someone moving a desert tortoise out of the road. It looked like flowers planted thirty years earlier and watered every morning by a woman who had no intention of moving.

A storm arrived after midnight. Thunder woke me first, followed by rain hammering against the hotel roof. I opened the window and smelled wet desert, an earthy scent so different from the dry dust of the previous day that the landscape seemed to have changed countries. Water poured from the gutters and ran along Main Street. Lightning illuminated the church, the filling station, and the empty school in brief white flashes. For nearly an hour San Rosario disappeared behind sheets of rain.

By sunrise the storm had moved east. The air was cool and startlingly clear. Water filled portions of the creek that had been dry the day before. Birds moved everywhere through the wet brush. The mountains no longer looked blue and distant; their ridges were sharp enough to see individual rock formations. I walked back to the railroad grade with my tripod and looked over the town again.

The same buildings were there, but I no longer saw them in quite the same way. Ruth's house was not merely one of the occupied structures. I knew about the garden behind it and the boots beside the door. The post office was no longer an anonymous adobe building. The school contained Gary's childhood and faded drawings made by children who were now adults somewhere else. The hotel porch held a photograph from 1951 and another, now stored on my camera, from the previous evening.

Gary's Jeep appeared behind me while I was setting up the tripod. He stopped long enough to remind me that Miguel needed help with the fence and apparently assumed that because I was still in town, I had volunteered. Then he drove away before I could object.

The morning sun reached the Hotel Rosario first. Its upper windows turned gold. Light moved gradually down the facade, crossed Main Street, and reached the roofs of the houses beyond it. For a few minutes the town seemed almost impossibly bright against the storm-dark mountains.

San Rosario was still disappearing. Nothing I had seen changed that fact. Twenty-five people could not sustain a school, a grocery store, a railroad, or the dozens of houses that had once filled the empty lots. Eventually there might be twenty people, then fifteen, then fewer. Perhaps one day nobody would replace the population sign because there would be nobody left to argue about the number.

But that morning the hotel was open. Ruth was probably already in her garden. Elena would drive in again on Friday to open the post office. Miguel had a fence to repair. Gary lived six miles outside town and would continue insisting that this counted.

For the moment, San Rosario was still there.

And for the moment, that was enough.
