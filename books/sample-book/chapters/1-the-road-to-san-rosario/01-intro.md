# Chapter 1 — The Road to San Rosario

There is a point, about forty miles after the last gas station, when the road stops pretending it is going anywhere important.

Until then, Route 18 behaves like any other highway in the American Southwest. It has painted lines, mile markers, occasional signs warning about cattle, and enough traffic to make you believe that somewhere ahead there must be a town worth reaching. Pickup trucks pass in the opposite direction. A refrigerated semi appears in the rearview mirror and disappears over the horizon. There are utility poles beside the road, marching dutifully toward whatever civilization waits beyond the next ridge.

Then, gradually, all of that falls away.

The utility poles turn east. The center line fades. The asphalt narrows until the shoulders are nothing more than gravel and desert sand. The radio loses the last country station from El Paso and replaces it with static.

For another twenty miles, there is almost nothing made by human hands.

This is the road to San Rosario.

I had first heard about the town from a photograph.

It was hanging behind the counter of a diner in Alpine, Texas, the sort of photograph that probably would have gone unnoticed if I hadn't been waiting fifteen minutes for a hamburger. The picture showed an old two-story hotel standing alone beneath an enormous sky. Half the windows were boarded over. A wooden balcony sagged across the front of the building, and above it, barely readable, were the words:

**HOTEL ROSARIO — ROOMS — COLD BEER**

The photograph was sun-bleached and crooked in its frame.

I asked the waitress where it had been taken.

"San Rosario," she said.

"Where's that?"

She pointed vaguely west with her coffee pot.

"Out there."

That was the entire explanation.

I asked whether the hotel was still open.

She laughed.

"No."

Then she reconsidered.

"Well. Depends what you mean by open."

That was enough.

Three days later, with two cameras, six lenses, a tripod, a cooler full of water, and a rental car whose agreement almost certainly prohibited what I was about to do with it, I turned off Route 18 and followed a battered green sign toward San Rosario.

Population 94.

Someone had crossed out the 94 with white paint.

Underneath it, in black marker, someone else had written:

**37**

I stopped to photograph the sign.

It seemed like the sort of thing I had come looking for.

---

The desert beyond the sign was greener than I expected.

From a distance, West Texas can look empty, but emptiness is mostly an illusion created by scale. Once you stop the car and walk into it, the landscape becomes crowded.

Creosote bushes filled the low ground. Clumps of yellow grass pushed through cracks in the earth. Ocotillo reached upward like bundles of crooked fingers. Every few hundred yards, a prickly pear cactus exploded into red fruit.

Mountains stood along the southern horizon, blue and indistinct in the afternoon haze.

Nothing moved.

Then a jackrabbit shot from beneath a bush and scared the hell out of me.

I watched it disappear across the road.

"All right," I said to nobody.

The rabbit did not apologize.

I got back into the car.

San Rosario appeared about fifteen minutes later.

There was no dramatic arrival. No cluster of buildings rising from the desert. No church steeple announcing the town from miles away.

Instead, pieces of it appeared one at a time.

First came a rusted windmill.

Then a fence.

Then the remains of what might once have been a filling station.

And finally, around a bend, the town itself.

Calling San Rosario a town required some generosity.

There were perhaps thirty buildings scattered across several dusty streets. Some were houses. Some had once been businesses. Others had deteriorated so completely that their original purpose was impossible to guess.

A white adobe church stood at the northern end of town.

Across from it was a school with plywood covering the windows.

Farther down the street, I saw the hotel from the photograph.

It was larger than I expected.

It occupied nearly an entire block, an improbable Victorian structure that looked as though someone had transported it from another century and abandoned it in the desert.

The balcony still sagged.

The sign was still there.

HOTEL ROSARIO.

And underneath:

ROOMS — COLD BEER.

I parked across the street.

For several minutes I simply sat in the car looking at it.

This was the photograph.

Only now there were details the photograph hadn't shown.

A bicycle leaned against the porch.

There was a pickup truck parked beside the building.

And one of the upstairs windows had an air conditioner sticking out of it.

Maybe the waitress hadn't been joking after all.

I grabbed my camera.

Before I had crossed the street, the front door opened.

An old man stepped onto the porch.

He was tall and painfully thin, wearing jeans, boots, and a white undershirt. His hair was silver and reached nearly to his shoulders.

He looked at me.

Then he looked at the camera.

"You're late," he said.

I stopped.

"Sorry?"

"The photographers usually come in April."

He sat down in a wooden chair.

"Better clouds."

I laughed.

"I didn't know there was a season."

"There's a season for everything."

He pointed toward another chair.

I climbed the steps and sat down.

The man's name was Walter.

He had lived in San Rosario for seventy-one years.

"I was born about two hundred yards from where you're sitting," he told me.

He pointed toward an empty lot.

"House is gone."

"What happened to it?"

"Gravity."

He said this with complete seriousness.

Then he smiled.

---

Walter told me that San Rosario had once had nearly twelve hundred residents.

There had been a copper mine in the mountains and a railroad spur connecting it to the main line farther north. The hotel opened in 1912. The school came five years later.

There had been three bars, two churches, a movie theater, a grocery store, a bank, and, according to Walter, "one very optimistic dentist."

The mine closed in 1958.

The railroad followed.

Then the town began the long process of disappearing.

Not all at once.

That would have been easier.

Instead, San Rosario lost people one family at a time.

Someone found work in Odessa.

Someone went to college in Austin.

Someone joined the Army.

Someone died.

Someone decided that driving eighty miles for groceries was ridiculous.

A house would go dark.

Then another.

The school held on until 1997, when enrollment dropped to eleven students.

After that, the remaining children rode a bus sixty-three miles each way.

"That's a hell of a school commute," I said.

Walter shrugged.

"Gives them time to do their homework."

I asked how many people lived in town now.

He looked toward the population sign.

"Thirty-seven is optimistic."

"So whoever changed the sign was wrong?"

"That was me."

"When?"

"2014."

I laughed.

"How many now?"

Walter thought about it.

"Twenty-six if you count everybody."

"What does that mean?"

"It means there's an argument about Gary."

I waited.

Walter offered no further explanation.

"Who's Gary?"

"Lives in an RV about six miles outside town."

"Doesn't that mean he doesn't live here?"

"That's my position."

Apparently the population of San Rosario was a matter of ongoing civic debate.

---

I asked about the hotel.

Walter turned and looked at the building as though he had forgotten it was behind him.

"My parents bought it in '62."

"You own it?"

"Unfortunately."

"Is it open?"

"Depends what you mean by open."

There it was again.

I told him about the waitress in Alpine.

Walter nodded.

"Probably Marlene."

He stood.

"Come on."

The lobby smelled like old wood, dust, and coffee.

There was a reception desk directly inside the door. Behind it hung a wooden board containing perhaps forty brass room keys.

Almost all of them were still there.

A ceiling fan rotated slowly overhead.

The furniture looked ancient but clean.

On one wall were photographs of San Rosario during its better years.

The same street outside had once been crowded with Model T Fords, horses, wagons, and men wearing hats.

The hotel balcony had been straight.

"What year is that?"

"1923."

I stepped closer.

There were more buildings in the photograph than existed in the entire town today.

Walter pointed toward a group of men standing outside the hotel.

"My grandfather's in there."

"Which one?"

"No idea."

I looked at him.

He laughed.

"Family history only gets you so far."

---

There were fourteen rooms upstairs.

Walter rented three of them.

Not regularly.

There was no website. No reservation system. No sign advertising vacancies.

People simply appeared.

Photographers. Bird watchers. Geologists. Motorcycle riders. Occasionally someone who had taken the wrong road and discovered San Rosario accidentally.

"How much is a room?"

"Forty dollars."

"That's it?"

"Breakfast isn't included."

"Is there breakfast?"

"No."

This seemed fair.

I asked if he had a room available.

Walter looked down the empty hallway.

"I think I can squeeze you in."

My room was number seven.

It contained a brass bed, a wooden dresser, a chair, a sink, and an air conditioner that sounded like a lawn mower trying to digest a wrench.

The bathroom was down the hall.

There was no television.

There was no Wi-Fi.

My phone displayed:

**NO SERVICE**

I put my bag on the bed.

For the first time in years, I realized that nobody on Earth could immediately contact me.

The sensation was unexpectedly wonderful.

---

At sunset I walked through town with my camera.

The light transformed everything.

Buildings that had looked exhausted at noon became beautiful.

Adobe walls glowed orange. Broken windows reflected the mountains. Long shadows stretched across empty lots where houses had once stood.

A dog followed me for several blocks.

He was brown, medium-sized, and completely uninterested in being photographed.

Every time I pointed the camera toward him, he turned away.

When I lowered it, he followed me again.

We developed an understanding.

At the edge of town I found the old railroad grade.

The tracks were gone, but the raised bed remained, cutting a perfectly straight line across the desert.

I climbed onto it.

From there, San Rosario looked impossibly small.

Twenty-six people.

Maybe twenty-seven if Gary counted.

The entire town fit easily inside the frame of my camera.

Behind it rose the mountains.

Above them, enormous thunderheads had begun gathering in the east.

Walter had been right.

Better clouds would have been nice.

But these would do.

I set up the tripod.

The sun dropped behind the mountains.

For perhaps thirty seconds, the sky turned red.

I pressed the shutter.

Once.

Twice.

Again.

Then the light disappeared.

The desert became blue.

Somewhere behind me, a coyote began calling.

Another answered.

Then another.

I packed the camera.

The dog was waiting beside the railroad grade.

"Hotel?" I asked.

He started walking.

Apparently so.

---

That night I sat with Walter on the hotel porch.

The temperature had dropped twenty degrees.

Someone had turned on a yellow light above the door, and moths battered themselves against it with suicidal determination.

Walter drank a beer.

I drank another.

The street remained empty.

"What keeps you here?" I asked.

He considered the question for a long time.

"Everybody asks that."

"I imagine."

"They think there has to be a reason."

"Does there?"

"No."

He took another drink.

"People live in Dallas because their job is there. People live in New York because they like New York. People live in some suburb because their wife liked the kitchen."

He pointed down the street.

"I live here."

That was apparently enough.

A light came on in a house two blocks away.

Walter nodded toward it.

"That's Ruth."

"One of the twenty-six?"

"Twenty-five."

"Someone leave?"

"Ruth's husband died in February."

"I'm sorry."

"So is she."

For a while neither of us spoke.

The desert was completely dark now.

Without streetlights, San Rosario seemed to dissolve into it.

Only a few windows remained illuminated.

Twenty-five people.

Maybe twenty-six with Gary.

I thought about the population sign.

At some point, somebody would have to change it again.

---

The next morning I woke before sunrise.

The hotel was silent.

I carried my camera downstairs and stepped onto the porch.

Walter was already there.

He handed me a cup of coffee.

"I thought breakfast wasn't included."

"It isn't."

I drank it anyway.

Across the street, the eastern sky was beginning to brighten.

The abandoned buildings slowly emerged from darkness.

A rooster crowed somewhere behind the church.

Then another answered.

Walter leaned against the porch railing.

"Where you going today?"

I told him I didn't know.

He nodded approvingly.

"Good."

The sun appeared over the desert.

And San Rosario began another day.
