# Chapter 1 — The Road to San Rosario

There is a point, about forty miles after the last gas station, when the road stops pretending it is going anywhere important. Until then, Route 18 behaves like any other highway crossing the American Southwest. It has painted lines, mile markers, occasional signs warning about cattle, and enough traffic to suggest that somewhere ahead there must be a town worth reaching. Pickup trucks pass in the opposite direction with their windows up against the heat. A refrigerated semi appears in the rearview mirror, follows for several miles, and eventually disappears over a low ridge. Utility poles stand beside the highway in patient succession, their wires drooping between them in shallow black curves. Then, almost imperceptibly, the evidence of civilization begins to fall away. The utility poles turn east. The center line fades. The shoulders narrow until asphalt gives way directly to gravel and desert sand. The radio loses the last station from El Paso, replacing a country song first with intermittent bursts of static and then with nothing at all. For the next twenty miles, the road runs alone through the desert.

This is the road to San Rosario.

I had first heard about the town from a photograph hanging behind the counter of a diner in Alpine, Texas. It was the sort of photograph I probably would never have noticed if I hadn't been waiting fifteen minutes for a hamburger. The picture showed an old two-story hotel beneath an enormous western sky. Half of its windows were boarded over, a wooden balcony sagged across the front of the building, and a faded sign above it announced HOTEL ROSARIO in letters that looked as though they had been painted when Theodore Roosevelt was president. Beneath the name, in smaller letters, were the words ROOMS — COLD BEER. The photograph itself had faded almost to sepia, and the cheap wooden frame hung slightly crooked. I asked the waitress where it had been taken. She pointed west with the coffee pot and said San Rosario was "out there somewhere." When I asked whether the hotel was still open, she laughed, considered the question for a moment, and said that depended on what I meant by open. It was exactly the kind of answer capable of rearranging a travel itinerary.

Three days later I turned off Route 18 with two cameras, six lenses, a tripod, enough bottled water to survive a minor emergency, and a rental car whose agreement almost certainly prohibited what I was doing to it. The road immediately deteriorated. Pavement gave way to patched pavement, which eventually surrendered to a surface that seemed to consist mostly of old asphalt held together by optimism. A battered green highway sign appeared beside the road after several miles. SAN ROSARIO, it announced, followed by POPULATION 94. Someone had painted over the number with white paint and written 37 underneath in black marker. I pulled onto the shoulder and photographed it. At the time it seemed like exactly the sort of thing I had come to find: a town disappearing so quickly that the official signs couldn't keep up.

The desert beyond the sign was greener than I expected. From a moving car, West Texas can look empty, but emptiness is mostly an illusion produced by scale. Once I stopped and walked a few yards from the road, the landscape became crowded with things I had failed to notice at sixty miles an hour. Creosote bushes occupied the low ground. Yellow grass pushed through cracks in the earth. Ocotillo rose above everything else in crooked bundles, and prickly pear cactus grew in broad patches covered with red fruit. Lizards vanished beneath rocks as I approached. Small birds moved invisibly through thorny shrubs, revealing themselves only through brief calls. Mountains stood along the southern horizon, softened by heat until they seemed almost translucent. The desert wasn't empty at all. It simply contained very little that had been made for people.

San Rosario revealed itself in fragments. First came a rusted windmill turning slowly beside a dry stock tank. Then a barbed-wire fence and a row of cottonwoods following the course of an invisible creek. The remains of a filling station appeared around the next bend, its pumps gone and its roof partially collapsed. A few houses followed, separated by empty lots where other houses had clearly once stood. Finally the road widened slightly and became Main Street, although there was no sign announcing the change. A white adobe church stood at the northern end of town, its bell tower bright against the blue sky. Across from it was a low school building with plywood covering several windows. Beyond that were perhaps two dozen houses, some carefully maintained and others slowly surrendering to weather. San Rosario had not vanished, but it had become porous. The desert had begun filling the spaces where the town used to be.

The Hotel Rosario stood near the center of everything. It was larger than the photograph had suggested, an improbable two-story structure with wooden columns, tall windows, and a balcony running almost the full length of the building. It looked as though it belonged beside a railroad station in 1915 rather than beside a nearly empty road in the twenty-first century. The balcony still sagged. The lettering was still visible. Yet there were details the old photograph had failed to prepare me for. A bicycle leaned against one of the porch columns. A pickup truck sat in the dirt beside the building. An air conditioner protruded from an upstairs window and dripped steadily onto the porch roof. Someone had swept the front steps. Whatever else could be said about the Hotel Rosario, it was not entirely abandoned.

Walter appeared before I reached the porch. He was tall and painfully thin, with silver hair touching his shoulders and the deeply weathered skin of someone who had spent most of his life outdoors. He looked first at me and then at the camera hanging from my shoulder, apparently placing me into a familiar category. Photographers, he explained, usually came in April because the clouds were better. I hadn't realized San Rosario had a photography season, but Walter assured me that everything had a season if you waited long enough. He had lived in town for seventy-one years and claimed to have been born about two hundred yards from where we were standing. The house was gone now. When I asked what had happened to it, he said simply, "Gravity," then sat down on the porch as if this constituted a complete architectural history.

Over the next hour, Walter gave me the abbreviated history of San Rosario. The town had once contained nearly twelve hundred people, most of them connected in some way to a copper mine in the mountains or the railroad spur built to serve it. The Hotel Rosario opened in 1912, followed within a few years by the school, a bank, a grocery store, several bars, two churches, a movie theater, and what Walter described as one very optimistic dentist. The mine closed in 1958. The railroad lasted a little longer, but eventually the spur was abandoned and the rails removed. San Rosario did not collapse immediately. Instead, it entered a slow decline measured not in disasters but in departures. A family moved to Odessa because there was work there. A teenager went to college in Austin and never came back. Someone joined the Army. Someone died. Someone decided that driving more than an hour for groceries was absurd. Each departure extinguished another porch light at night.

The school survived until 1997, when enrollment fell to eleven students. After that, the remaining children rode a bus to another district sixty-three miles away. The grocery store had already closed. The filling station followed. The post office survived on reduced hours, largely because nobody had yet completed whatever bureaucratic procedure was required to kill it. Walter estimated that the town's current population was twenty-five, although there was apparently an unresolved dispute concerning a man named Gary who lived six miles outside town in a travel trailer. Walter maintained that six miles outside town was, by definition, not town. Gary's position, which I would hear at considerable length the following day, relied heavily on the fact that his mailing address said San Rosario. The population sign showing thirty-seven turned out to be Walter's work. He had changed it himself in 2014 and had never bothered updating it again.

The hotel had belonged to Walter's family since 1962. His parents bought it after the previous owner decided there was little future in operating a hotel in a town that was rapidly running out of both visitors and residents. Somehow the business never completely died. Walter rented three of the fourteen rooms, although "rented" implied a degree of organization that didn't exist. There was no website, no reservation system, no listing on the usual travel sites, and no sign advertising vacancies. People simply appeared. Bird watchers came during migration season. Geologists came to examine the mountains. Motorcycle riders discovered the place because they had taken the scenic route. Photographers came in April for the clouds. Occasionally someone arrived because they were lost. A room cost forty dollars, breakfast not included, mostly because there was no breakfast.

Inside, the hotel smelled of old wood, dust, coffee, and something faintly metallic that might have come from the ancient plumbing. The lobby contained a wooden reception desk and a board holding dozens of brass room keys, nearly all of them still hanging from their hooks. A ceiling fan turned overhead with the slow determination of a machine that had been performing the same task for decades. The furniture was old but clean. Along one wall hung photographs of San Rosario in its prosperous years. In a picture dated 1923, Main Street was crowded with Model T Fords, horses, wagons, children, and men wearing dark suits despite what must have been ferocious heat. Buildings occupied nearly every lot. The Hotel Rosario's balcony was straight. Walter pointed toward a group of men gathered beneath it and said his grandfather was probably among them, although nobody in the family had ever determined which one he was.

Room seven contained a brass bed, a dresser, one wooden chair, a porcelain sink, and a window air conditioner that sounded like a lawn mower trying to digest a wrench. The bathroom was down the hall. There was no television and no Wi-Fi. My phone displayed NO SERVICE. I put my bag on the bed and stood for a moment looking at those two words. At home they would have been an inconvenience. Here they felt strangely luxurious. For the first time in years, nobody could immediately contact me. No notification could demand an answer. No email could quietly rearrange the next hour. Whatever was happening elsewhere would have to continue happening without my participation.

I spent the late afternoon walking through town with my camera. The hard white light of midday softened as the sun moved toward the mountains, and buildings that had seemed merely exhausted began to look beautiful. Adobe walls turned gold. Broken windows reflected the sky. Rusted metal caught the light in thin orange lines. Empty lots became fields of yellow grass. A brown dog appeared near the church and followed me for several blocks while refusing absolutely to be photographed. Whenever I raised the camera, he turned his head or walked behind something. Whenever I lowered it, he resumed following. By the time we reached the edge of town, we had reached an understanding in which I stopped trying to photograph him and he agreed not to leave.

Beyond the last houses I found the old railroad grade. The tracks had disappeared decades ago, but the raised bed remained, running in a perfectly straight line through the desert. I climbed onto it and looked back. From there, the entire town fit easily within the frame of my camera. The hotel rose above the surrounding buildings. The church stood at one end. The school sat silent near the other. Between them were occupied houses, abandoned houses, empty lots, trees, sheds, water tanks, old cars, and the accumulated evidence of more than a century of people trying to establish permanence in a place that seemed naturally resistant to the idea.

The sun disappeared behind the mountains shortly after eight. For less than a minute the western sky turned brilliant red, illuminating the undersides of clouds that had gathered during the afternoon. I set up the tripod and photographed the town as quickly as I could, changing exposure between frames while the light faded. Then the color disappeared almost at once. The desert became blue, the mountains became black, and the first lights appeared in San Rosario. There weren't many. A porch light came on near the church. A kitchen window glowed several streets away. The hotel sign was barely visible. Somewhere behind me a coyote called, followed by another farther south.

That night Walter and I sat on the hotel porch beneath a yellow light while moths threw themselves repeatedly against the glass fixture. The temperature had fallen enough that the heat stored in the wooden boards beneath our chairs felt pleasant. Walter talked about the town without sentimentality. He did not describe its decline as a tragedy, nor did he seem particularly interested in preserving San Rosario as a monument to itself. People had left because they had reasons to leave. Others remained because they had reasons to stay, or perhaps because leaving had never seemed necessary. Outsiders, he said, always wanted an explanation for why anyone still lived there. They expected a story involving family duty, stubbornness, nostalgia, or some profound attachment to the land. Walter's explanation was much simpler. He lived in San Rosario because San Rosario was where he lived.

Long after dark, the town seemed to dissolve into the desert. Without streetlights, the boundaries between settlement and wilderness disappeared. The hotel porch became a small island of yellow light surrounded by darkness. Somewhere down the street a screen door slammed. A dog barked twice and stopped. The wind moved through the cottonwoods near the dry creek. Everything else was silent. I thought again about the population sign and its obsolete correction. Thirty-seven had once seemed astonishingly small. Now even that number was history.

I woke before sunrise the following morning and carried my camera downstairs. Walter was already on the porch with coffee. The eastern horizon had begun to brighten, revealing the buildings one by one. The abandoned filling station appeared first as a silhouette, then the school, then the church. Birds began moving through the trees. A rooster crowed somewhere beyond Main Street. The town emerged slowly from darkness, not as a ruin or a ghost town but as a place beginning another ordinary day.

I had arrived expecting to photograph what was disappearing. Standing there in the cold blue light, I began to suspect that the more interesting subject might be what remained.
